# Tightened background draft for Overleaf

Cardiovascular AI has produced many accurate static prediction models, but much less progress has been made on the clinically harder question of individualized decision-making over time. In practice, clinicians do not only ask who is high risk. They ask who is likely to benefit from intervention, who requires closer surveillance, and who can be safely spared unnecessary follow-up. That distinction is central to this proposal. The opportunity is to move from cross-sectional risk estimation toward longitudinal, multimodal, patient-specific decision support that is explicit about uncertainty, transportability, and implementation.

This direction is increasingly supported by the broader literature. A 2023 scoping review of AI-based precision cardiovascular medicine found 28 studies, but most remained focused on prediction rather than treatment planning or dynamic surveillance, with prediction accounting for 50% of included studies and EHRs used in 79% (PMID: 37623518). More generally, a 2025 systematic review of reinforcement learning and dynamic treatment regimes in healthcare identified 46 studies and concluded that adaptive, individualized decision support is now a recognized methodological direction, even though interpretability, reward definition, data quality, and clinician adoption remain major barriers (PMID: 40724777). In cardiovascular medicine specifically, digital-twin reviews are converging on the same broad vision of multimodal, individualized, dynamically updated care, but often at a level of abstraction that is still too distant from deployable NHS decision support (PMID: 39322420). The practical opportunity is therefore not merely to predict events more accurately, but to build decision-grade longitudinal models that can be clinically tested.

The AF ablation use case gives the clearest rationale for individualized treatment-effect estimation. In CABANA, the primary composite endpoint occurred in 8.0% of patients randomized to ablation and 9.2% randomized to drug therapy, HR 0.86 (95% CI 0.65 to 1.15), showing that the average intention-to-treat effect was mixed. Yet ablation reduced death or cardiovascular hospitalization, 51.7% vs 58.1%, HR 0.83 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.93), and markedly reduced AF recurrence, 49.9% vs 69.5%, HR 0.52 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.60) (PMID: 30874766). More importantly for this proposal, newer CABANA analyses show explicit heterogeneity of benefit: in patients with high comorbidity burden, ablation was associated with a primary-outcome HR of 0.62 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.93), compared with 1.16 (95% CI 0.76 to 1.77) in those with low comorbidity burden, interaction P=0.038 (PMID: 41213867). This is exactly the setting in which individualized treatment-effect modelling is more clinically meaningful than another generic AF risk score.

The inherited-cardiomyopathy workstream provides a complementary surveillance-intensity problem. Screening remains necessary, but current policies are still coarse. In dilated cardiomyopathy, screening of 1,365 first-degree relatives identified new DCM-related findings in 14.1%, including 2.1% with DCM itself (PMID: 37225358). In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the baseline yield of family screening is high, but the tail of repeated follow-up is much lower. In 1,230 relatives from 531 families, baseline combined clinical and genetic yield was 26%, whereas only 43 additional relatives developed HCM over 6,762 person-years. In one low-risk gene-elusive subgroup, only 0.4% developed HCM on follow-up (PMID: 39365224). Current HCM guidance still relies on broad categorical intervals, for example 1 to 2 years, 2 to 3 years, or 3 to 5 years depending on age and family context. This makes inherited cardiomyopathy a particularly strong setting for trajectory-based risk-adapted surveillance.

The post-MI workstream is now supported by a broader evidence base than is often acknowledged, although the field remains dominated by one-off prognostic models rather than dynamic care policies. NICE already frames acute coronary syndromes as requiring both early and longer-term rehabilitation management. A 2025 systematic review of AI in acute myocardial ischemia included 30 studies across diagnosis, risk stratification, and decision support (PMID: 41523481). A 2025 meta-analysis of machine-learning models for major cardiovascular events after MI included 28 studies covering 59,392 patients and reported pooled validation performance of C-index 0.77, sensitivity 0.78, and specificity 0.85 (PMID: 40630448). A 2026 STEMI study achieved an AUC of 0.821 using only five variables for 1-year mortality prediction (PMID: 41688111). Together, these data support post-MI care as a plausible and policy-relevant use case, but also show that the next step should be time-updated follow-up and treatment optimization rather than another static outcome model.

A major enabling modality for this programme is AI-enhanced ECG. The academic AI-ECG literature has already shown that low-cost physiological signals can encode latent structural and hemodynamic disease. Landmark work on low ejection fraction achieved AUC 0.93 with sensitivity 86.3% and specificity 85.7% in an independent test set of 52,870 patients, and a positive AI-ECG was associated with 4.1-fold higher future risk of ventricular dysfunction (PMID: 30617318). AI-ECG has since been extended to AF detection during sinus rhythm (PMID: 31378392), HCM detection with AUC 0.96 (PMID: 32081280), cardiac amyloidosis with AUC 0.91 and prediction more than 6 months before diagnosis in many cases (PMID: 34218880), pulmonary hypertension with AUC 0.88 and prognostic value for mortality (PMID: 36338407), and external aortic stenosis detection with strong negative predictive value (PMID: 40703138). However, a 2023 systematic review found that only 34% of clinically relevant ECG deep-learning models had external validation and only 11% made implementation resources available (PMID: 38288263). Thus, AI-ECG should be presented in this proposal as a scalable phenotyping and screening layer, not as a self-sufficient translational success story.

Commercial activity reinforces that interpretation. AliveCor, Anumana, and Philips/Cardiologs all now market AI-ECG products emphasizing workflow integration, regulatory status, and scale. This is strategically useful because it shows translational demand and an existing deployment pathway. But these commercial examples should be treated as evidence of market direction and implementation architecture, not as substitutes for independent validation.

The literature on large language models is also relevant, but in a narrower way. Current evidence is strongest for procedure-adjacent functions such as patient education, consent-support drafting, readability improvement, summarization, and checklist-style perioperative support. For example, comparative evaluation in anesthesiology showed that LLM performance varies materially by model and topic even when answers are scored by senior specialists (PMID: 41899118). Interventional-radiology studies suggest that LLMs can improve patient-facing documents and produce readable consent-support text, but still leave many clinicians uneasy about completeness (PMIDs: 41052822, 39612047). A spine-surgery study found that LLMs were more credible for broad surgical versus non-surgical triage than for actual procedure selection (PMID: 41424195). A 2025 systematic review in anesthesiology and critical care concluded that LLMs are not yet equipped to fully assist physicians in these settings (PMID: 40524117). Accordingly, LLMs belong in this programme, if at all, as supervised communication and workflow tools rather than as autonomous procedural guides.

From the perspective of translation, patient trust and policy realism are decisive. Patients in heart-failure care were much more comfortable with clinicians using AI support than with AI acting independently, and 97.3% said they would trust their cardiologist over AI in case of disagreement (PMID: 41346424). UK policy is similarly receptive but conditional: NICE, MHRA, FDA, and NHS guidance all point toward evidence proportionate to risk, lifecycle governance, subgroup performance scrutiny, and explicit implementation pathways. This means the strongest version of the proposal is not a general claim that AI can improve cardiology. It is a clear programme to build and validate a generalizable, NHS-ready framework for individualized decisions about intervention, surveillance, and follow-up intensity, using multimodal longitudinal data, scalable physiological signals such as ECG, and carefully bounded clinician-supervised workflow support.
