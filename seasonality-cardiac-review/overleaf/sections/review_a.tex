\subsection*{Direct evidence from cardiac imaging}

The direct literature on seasonality of imaging-defined cardiac structure and function is small. The clearest repeated-imaging studies are in athlete echocardiography, where loading conditions and training phase vary naturally across the year. Early work in cyclists and collegiate basketball athletes showed seasonal changes in ventricular dimensions, stroke volume, and left ventricular mass across the training or competition cycle \cite{Fagard1983,Crouse1992}. More recent studies suggest that the seasonal signal may be more obvious in load-sensitive or mechanics-sensitive phenotypes than in gross chamber size alone. In elite skiers, right-heart filling and pulmonary ejection-time measures differed between winter and summer despite relatively stable chamber dimensions \cite{Dernovoj2022}. In elite rugby league athletes, conventional structure and function were fairly stable across the competitive year, whereas left-ventricular twist and apical rotation varied with season and training load \cite{Forsythe2023}.

Taken together, these studies suggest two points relevant to CMR. First, the most detectable seasonal effect may not be in simple volume or mass measurements, but in more sensitive functional or mechanical phenotypes. Second, any observed seasonality may reflect a mixture of environmental exposure, behaviour, training, and physiology rather than a pure photoperiod-driven biological rhythm.

\subsection*{Limited direct CMR evidence and related contextual studies}

The CMR literature is much thinner and is concentrated in acute coronary syndromes rather than stable longitudinal imaging cohorts. In a STEMI cohort studied with post-PCI CMR, season was not associated with infarct size, area at risk, myocardial salvage index, or microvascular obstruction \cite{Park2019}. In a larger pooled analysis of six randomized STEMI trials, season again was not independently associated with infarct size, microvascular obstruction, or left-ventricular ejection fraction, but ambient temperature extremes were associated with larger infarcts and lower ejection fraction \cite{Redfors2022}. These studies do not establish seasonality of stable cardiac phenotype remodeling, but they do show that time-varying environmental context can influence CMR-derived endpoints.

This distinction is important for a three-year CMR cohort. A null finding for coarse season categories does not imply that imaging measurements are temporally invariant. It may instead indicate that month or season is too crude a representation of the relevant exposure, which could involve temperature, daylight duration, infection burden, physical activity, or other environmental covariates.

\subsection*{Broader cardiovascular context and biological plausibility}

Beyond direct imaging, the surrounding cardiovascular literature provides strong biological plausibility for annual variation in cardiac phenotype expression. Repeated-measures physiology studies show winter shifts toward lower cardiac output or stroke volume and higher vascular resistance \cite{Radke2010}. Population and hospital studies show marked winter excess in heart-failure admissions and mortality \cite{Boulay1999,Gallerani2011}. Reviews of environmental drivers of heart-failure decompensation emphasize temperature and other ambient exposures as clinically meaningful triggers \cite{Mete2019,Bhatia2017}. This broader evidence does not prove that general-population CMR phenotypes vary by season, but it makes the hypothesis plausible.

\subsection*{Implications for CMR studies recorded over three years}

For CMR studies recorded at different months across three years, the current literature suggests that any real seasonal signal is likely to be subtle and phenotype-specific. The most plausible hypothesis-generating targets are strain, atrial or right-heart measures, and shape or mechanics descriptors rather than only gross chamber size. More speculative targets, such as tissue-characterization phenotypes, should be framed cautiously unless stronger direct supporting evidence is added. At the same time, the sparse direct evidence means that confounding remains a major concern. Apparent seasonality could be induced by scanner upgrades, protocol drift, acquisition-year imbalance, or operational scheduling patterns if those factors co-vary with calendar time.

The evidence review therefore supports a cautious framing: seasonality in CMR is plausible and scientifically interesting, but it cannot be assumed. The correct goal is a controlled analysis that can separate small annual biological effects from technical and organisational artefact.
