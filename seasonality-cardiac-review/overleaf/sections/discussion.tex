The central conclusion of this review is that the methodological literature is already strong enough to support a rigorous seasonality analysis in CMR, even though the direct imaging literature remains sparse. That combination creates a useful scientific opportunity. A well-controlled CMR study recorded across different months and years is not merely repeating an established result; it has genuine potential to clarify whether annual variation exists in imaging-defined cardiac structure, function, mechanics, or tissue phenotype.

The evidence review suggests that the strongest direct precedents come from repeated echocardiographic studies in athletes, where at least some measures vary with season or training phase \cite{Fagard1983,Crouse1992,Dernovoj2022,Forsythe2023}. The CMR literature is currently more indirect and more disease-specific, with acute STEMI studies suggesting that ambient conditions may matter even when simple season categories do not \cite{Park2019,Redfors2022}. The broader cardiovascular literature adds plausibility by showing seasonal variation in haemodynamics, vascular resistance, heart-failure burden, and environmental triggers \cite{Radke2010,Boulay1999,Gallerani2011,Mete2019}.

For the planned three-year CMR dataset, the most defensible default is therefore not a simple comparison of winter versus summer means, but phenotype-level cyclic regression with explicit adjustment for calendar year and technical covariates. That approach aligns the statistical model with the actual data-generating process: irregular scan dates, multiple years, heterogeneous participants, and potentially strong scanner or protocol confounding \cite{Stolwijk1999,Cornelissen2014,Wood2017}.

There are also clear limitations. The direct evidence base is still dominated by non-CMR modalities and selected cohorts. Some included literature is represented by metadata and abstract-level extraction rather than full publication-grade full-text review. Coarse season labels may be weaker than more mechanistic exposures such as temperature, daylight, training load, or recent illness. Finally, true seasonal effects in CMR, if present, may be small enough that negative findings could arise from limited power or imperfect control of technical variability.

Even with those caveats, the path forward is clear. A high-quality CMR seasonality study should prespecify the cyclic model, audit acquisition imbalance before inference, report amplitude and phase rather than only significance, and use sensitivity analyses to separate annual biological structure from environmental or operational artefact. If such a study identifies a reproducible signal, it would have implications for longitudinal study design, follow-up scheduling, and interpretation of small between-timepoint differences. If it identifies no meaningful signal, that negative result would be equally valuable because it would help rule out an important source of hidden variability in repeat CMR studies.
