Seasonal variation in cardiovascular physiology and clinical events is well established, with recurrent winter excess in blood pressure, vascular resistance, heart-failure admissions, and cardiovascular mortality \cite{Radke2010,Boulay1999,Gallerani2011,Bhatia2017,Mete2019}. By contrast, direct evidence for seasonality in imaging-defined cardiac structure and function is much less mature. That distinction matters for cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR): if seasonal effects are present in CMR-derived phenotypes, they may represent a genuine and under-recognized source of biological and technical variation rather than a settled feature that is already routinely handled in imaging studies.

The direct imaging literature currently comes mostly from repeated echocardiographic studies in athletes rather than from general-population CMR. Classic and contemporary athlete studies report seasonal or training-phase changes in chamber dimensions, stroke volume, right-heart function, or myocardial mechanics, although the signal is not uniform across all phenotypes \cite{Fagard1983,Crouse1992,Dernovoj2022,Forsythe2023}. This literature suggests that at least some imaging-derived cardiac measurements can vary over the year, especially when loading conditions, training intensity, temperature, or daylight-associated behavior also change.

Direct CMR evidence is much thinner. Existing CMR studies are largely confined to disease cohorts, especially ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), rather than stable longitudinal imaging cohorts. In one study, season itself was not associated with infarct size or myocardial salvage on post-PCI CMR \cite{Park2019}. In a larger pooled CMR analysis, coarse season labels again were not independently associated with infarct severity, but ambient temperature extremes were associated with larger infarct size and lower left ventricular ejection fraction \cite{Redfors2022}. These STEMI studies therefore do not establish seasonality in stable cardiac remodelling or general-population CMR phenotypes. Instead, they suggest that environmental exposure may matter even when simple season categories do not, and they highlight the need to distinguish biological seasonality from more specific weather-related effects.

For a CMR dataset containing scans recorded in different months across three years, the key practical question is therefore not only whether a seasonality signal exists, but also how it should be assessed. A naive comparison of winter versus summer risks conflating biology with scanner drift, protocol updates, year effects, and operational scheduling. The more defensible strategy is to review the sparse direct imaging evidence, review the mature seasonality-methods literature, and then build a statistical framework tailored to irregularly timed CMR phenotypes.

This manuscript therefore has three goals. First, it reviews the evidence for seasonality in cardiac structure, function, and related cardiovascular phenotypes, with emphasis on what is directly informative for CMR. Second, it reviews the main statistical approaches for detecting seasonality in irregularly timed biomedical data. Third, it proposes a practical analysis framework for studying seasonality effects in CMR images and derived phenotypes measured at different months across a three-year period.
